# The Dynamic Dialectic of the Evolution of Knowledge Systems: On "Change" and "Invariance" in Theoretical Identity

## Abstract

Set against the backdrop of rapid knowledge iteration and the technological transformation wrought by artificial intelligence, and responding to the epistemological anxiety of contemporary society, this paper explores the dialectical relationship between "change" and "invariance" in the evolution of knowledge systems. It integrates analytic philosophy's critique of exceptionalism about logic, the sociology of science's reflection on the Mertonian norms, and the conception of truth in Marxist historical materialism, and it draws on the hard-core/protective-belt structure of Lakatos's "scientific research programmes" in an attempt to dispel the static, absolutely a priori conception of truth while avoiding the trap of relativism. The study argues that the "invariance" of a theoretical system does not consist in frozen, specific propositions but is manifested as an underlying cognitive disposition, an objective material foundation, and a core value anchor; its auxiliary secondary rules and peripheral norms, by contrast, must absorb empirical shocks and the anomalies of the age through an adaptive "change." Taking the repair mechanism of Russell's paradox and the reconstruction of the Mertonian norms in the post-academic era as representative cases, this paper attempts to construct an evolutionary model of "revising secondary rules so as to maintain core identity," and offers a preliminary response to the theoretical difficulty of "how to distinguish the core from the periphery." The model helps to establish, in a world of uncertainty, a theoretical identity that is at once flexible and stable.

*Keywords:* anti-exceptionalism about logic; change and invariance; cognitive disposition; Marxism; theoretical identity

## Introduction

In today's era of rapid knowledge iteration, humanity is undergoing a reconstruction of its informational landscape at an unprecedented pace. The rise of deep learning and of large language models is reshaping our modes of material production and forms of social organization, and it poses new challenges to the traditional mechanisms of knowledge justification and to our cognitive paradigms. In this modern society—described by sociologists as "liquid"—human beings inevitably confront a profound epistemological anxiety. One root of this anxiety lies in the fact that the currently dominant methods of deep learning are essentially "black-box systems" that lack inferential interpretability. When machine algorithms begin profoundly to shape human belief, to determine the distribution of information, and even to generate so-called "knowledge," the traditional mechanisms of truth-verification grounded in logical deduction and empirical confirmation face a measure of risk of failure. The debate over explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) is not merely an engineering problem but a profound philosophical one: it concerns our inquiry into the very nature of explanation, and the question of how to preserve epistemic responsibility in the face of information overload and cognitive burden.

Above this continually collapsing and rebuilding epistemic situation, does there still exist a relatively stable bedrock of truth? If all knowledge, all theories, and even the underlying laws of logic are fluid and adjustable, will we not slide into the abyss of relativism or nihilism, and thereby lose the cornerstone for establishing scientific conviction and theoretical identity? For a long time, in order to ward off the existential anxiety that uncertainty brings, human beings have tended to fall into a binary opposition at the epistemological level. On the one hand, traditional foundationalist conceptions are inclined to regard formal logic and pure mathematics as absolutely invariant a priori oracles, treating them as "enclaves" independent of the empirical world. On the other hand, with respect to grand social-historical theories such as Marxism, people are prone, in the course of accepting them, to lapse into a vulgarized dogmatism, treating conclusions that were specific to particular historical conditions as absolute dogmas valid everywhere and for all time. Yet the actual trajectory of the history of science and of thought repeatedly demonstrates that a static conception of truth—one that absolutely severs "change" from "invariance"—can neither effectively explain the dynamic process by which knowledge evolves, nor avoid pushing people, once the myth of "absolute invariance" has been shattered, toward an epistemological nihilism in which "everything can be deconstructed."

To break this binary opposition, we must re-examine the dialectical relationship between "change" and "invariance" in theoretical evolution. In analytic philosophy, ever since Quine proposed the "web of belief," the absolute boundary between "analytic" and "synthetic" propositions has been broken down, and logic and experience have been placed within one and the same dynamic cognitive network. Timothy Williamson has further advanced and defended "anti-exceptionalism about logic," arguing that logic is not exceptional among the sciences and that to accept a new logic is essentially a special case of accepting a new scientific theory. This means that even the laws of logic, as the bedrock of knowledge, cannot be wholly immune to experience; defeasible inferential dispositions play an important role in theory acceptance and are adjusted as the evidence changes. At the same time, from the perspective of the sociology of science—as reflections on the rationality of the Mertonian norms reveal—in the post-academic era we must both uphold the "invariance" of the basic spirit of science and, through the "change" of external norms, adapt to a complex social context. And within the Marxist horizon, truth has never been an abstract, supra-historical entity but is concrete and historical; the vitality of the basic principles of Marxism lies precisely in its capacity, while holding fast to the core standpoint of materialist dialectics, to absorb fresh nourishment from the age through ceaseless social practice.

Within this lineage, Imre Lakatos's "methodology of scientific research programmes" offers a structural model especially worth drawing upon. Lakatos held that a mature scientific theory is not an isolated proposition but a research programme jointly constituted by a "hard core" and a "protective belt": the hard core consists of the core assumptions that the community, in long-term practice, in fact refuses to abandon, while the protective belt is composed of revisable auxiliary hypotheses that bear the function of absorbing anomalies and protecting the hard core. The argument of this paper draws critically on this structure and extends it at the intersection of three theoretical paths—analytic philosophy, the sociology of science, and Marxism: the "invariance" of a theory need not be presupposed as given a priori but can be understood as that part which, across successive crises, manifests the strongest resistance to revision and is anchored by value commitments.

On the basis of this theoretical background, this paper attempts to break the static myth of absolute truth in traditional epistemology and to explore the inner mechanism by which knowledge systems and theoretical constructions maintain "self-identity" and achieve "self-renewal" when confronted with the anomalies of the age. The core thesis is this: the "invariance" of any mature theory is not embodied in a rigid adherence to its specific formulas, propositions, applications, or policies, but is manifested rather as the anchoring of a resilient underlying "cognitive disposition" and a basic value standpoint; its "change," by contrast, consists in the continual adjustment and evolution that the theory must carry out at its periphery in order to cope with the impact of the empirical world and to resolve crises of anomaly.

The paper unfolds along this line of thought. First, by integrating analytic philosophy's critique of the apriority of logic with the Marxist account of the historicity of truth, it deconstructs the conception of absolutely invariant truth. Second, it analyzes the substance of "invariance" within the theoretical core—namely, its resilience as a defeasible cognitive disposition and basic standpoint—and attempts to answer the theoretical difficulty of how to distinguish the core from the periphery. It then takes Russell's paradox in the history of mathematics and the process of its repair as a case and, combining this with Lakatos's hard-core/protective-belt structure, constructs a gradualist evolutionary model of "a stable core and a sensitive periphery." Finally, it explores the insights this dynamic dialectic offers for guarding against contemporary dogmatism and the trap of relativism, and for re-establishing a resilient theoretical identity in an uncertain modern world.

## The Myth of "Absolute Invariance" in Theoretical Evolution

The history of theoretical evolution is, in a sense, also a history of bidding farewell to the myth of "absolute invariance." To understand this process, we must begin with Kant's transcendental construction. Through his "Copernican revolution," Kant sought, in the wake of Hume's skepticism, to rebuild the necessity of knowledge, regarding pure general logic as the science of the laws of all thinking. In the preface to the second edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Kant judged that logic, since Aristotle, had already entered upon the secure path of a science, having taken not a single step forward and so appearing to be closed and complete. In Kant's system, categories such as causality, substance, unity, and plurality are "molds" innately possessed within the human cognitive faculty. This transcendental cognitive bedrock constitutes the necessary precondition for the human transformation of sensible intuition into objective experience.

Kant's transcendental fortress was, for more than a century thereafter, regarded as an unbridgeable chasm—until, in the mid-twentieth century, Quine launched a fundamental challenge to it in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." The logical positivists had attempted to reduce mathematics and logic to linguistic tautologies, thereby defending their absolute invariance. But Quine argued that such a defense relies on a dogma that has never been rigorously justified: namely, that there exists an absolute boundary between "analytic" and "synthetic" propositions. Through logical reasoning Quine deconstructed this boundary—the so-called analyticity of being "true by meaning alone" ultimately collapses into a circular argument over the definition of synonymy. On this basis Quine proposed the "web of belief": the entire body of human knowledge and belief, from the most peripheral facts of geography and history to the most central laws of quantum mechanics and even the laws of pure mathematics and logic, together constitutes a seamless, integrated field of force. The edges of this field collide directly with the empirical world, while its center consists of the laws of formal logic. As Quine put it, no statement is immune to revision. When peripheral empirical observations encounter a strong anomaly, then in order to eliminate logical contradictions within the entire belief system the theorist may adopt various strategies of adjustment: revising the peripheral observations, proposing new auxiliary hypotheses, or even, in extreme cases—if revising the central laws of logic can restore the system's equilibrium in the simplest and most effective way—adjusting the laws of logic themselves in order to cope with the empirical impact.

Quine explained, by means of the "principle of minimum mutilation," why logic appears "absolutely invariant" in everyday life: this is not because it possesses some transcendent privilege, but because revising the logical laws at the center of the web implicates too wide a range and carries too high a cost, sending violent tremors throughout the entire scientific system. The "invariance" of logic is thus a strategic stability grounded in systemic pragmatism, not a rigid a priori absolute. It is worth noting that the discussion, in the development of quantum mechanics, of the classical distributive law of logic (that is, the rise of so-called "quantum logic"), though often invoked as a case supporting Quine's theory, remains philosophically contested in the scholarly community—Putnam himself later modified his early position to some extent. This paper treats it as an important reference point for Quinean holism rather than as a decisive confirmation.

After Quine, Williamson further advanced the argument for "anti-exceptionalism about logic." It should first be noted that Williamson's central claim does not directly correspond to a "core–periphery" hierarchical model; rather, it emphasizes the continuity in epistemological status between logic and scientific theory: accepting a logical theory and accepting a scientific theory belong methodologically to the same kind of abduction, their difference being one of degree rather than of kind. This paper draws on Williamson's insight into this continuity and, in what follows, combines it with a Lakatosian structural model—a deliberate synthesis rather than a restatement of Williamson's own original intent.

To break the superstition of logic's absolute invariance, Williamson introduces, by way of contrast, the distinction between "folk physics" and "scientific physics." Just as human beings possess an instinctive, pre-reflective folk physics, so too do they possess an innate "folk logic." Folk logic comprises the heuristics by which we carry out simple inferences in everyday life; it is efficient but not absolutely reliable. In folk logic, for example, people may take it for granted that from "every F is G" one may infer "some F is G," yet under the reflection of modern scientific logic this instinctive inference is exposed as invalid the moment it meets the case of an empty subject term. Therefore, just as scientific physics can, through reflection, overturn and correct folk physics, so scientific logic can likewise overturn and correct folk logic. The process of accepting a logical theory is, in essence, a process in which scientific logic is continually revised and iterated; it is no frozen, unchanging oracle.

In the face of the charge of "change," the late Quine offered a retreating view: to accept a new logic (such as quantum logic or intuitionistic logic) is merely to "change the subject" rather than to "deny the doctrine," because the new logic assigns the logical vocabulary an entirely different meaning. Williamson, however, refutes this descriptivist strategy of dissolving theoretical "change" into semantic change. He points out that alternative logicians (such as intuitionists) level a genuine charge against classical logic: they really do hold that classical logicians "fallaciously" employ the law of excluded middle. This neglect of the social dimension of meaning is analogous to the mistaken descriptivist account of proper names and natural-kind terms. The dispute between classical and non-classical logicians is therefore a genuine disagreement. The change of a logical theory, like the theoretical revolutions of the natural sciences, is decided by evaluating the explanatory power, simplicity, and strength of competing theories in coping with knowledge crises. Defeasible inferential dispositions play an important role in the acceptance of theories in logic and mathematics, and such dispositions change as the evidence changes.

For Hegel, "the truth is the whole"; it is not a static result but a process of self-negating movement that continually goes out of itself, negates itself, and returns to itself. Hegel introduced the concept of "sublation" (*Aufhebung*): the process of preserving the rational element of a prior stage and raising it into a higher unity even as its limitation is negated. On the surface, Hegel's dialectic is dynamic. The limitation of Hegelian philosophy, however, lies in the fact that the operation of its vast system is driven by an a priori posited "Absolute Spirit." The flux of history and the succession of theories are understood as the process by which Absolute Spirit comes to know itself in time. When Absolute Spirit attains complete self-knowledge, history reaches its end, and truth converges upon a perfect, closed "absolute knowing." This means that within Hegel's system all "change" ultimately serves only that pre-established "absolute invariance" which will finally congeal into eternity. The conservatism of the system thus, to a certain extent, overwhelms the revolutionary force of the dialectic in the end.

Marx's critique of Hegel is, in essence, a disenchantment of this closed ontology. He acutely pointed out that the so-called "Absolute Spirit" is nothing but a speculative phantom projected by the alienation of humanity's essential powers. When Marx restored the true subject of history to "real individuals" and their sensuous activity, the supra-experiential and static rhetoric of "absolute invariance" lost its foundation. The constancy of a theory thereby no longer congeals in the mechanical recitation of specific doctrinal articles, but settles into a firm adherence to the fundamental value standpoint of human emancipation and to the materialist-dialectical method. As a cognitive system with openness built into it, Marxism has always been committed to drawing empirical nourishment from new eras in order to accomplish its own remolding; this adaptive "change," far from dissolving its inner identity, has rather, through the continual sublation of outworn forms, brought about the trans-epochal leap of the theoretical core.

Within the horizon of historical materialism, truth is endowed with concreteness and historicity. Marxism holds that the specific conclusions of any theoretical system are not once-and-for-all dogmas but reflections of the contradictory movement of productive forces and relations of production at a particular historical stage. As practice deepens, as science and technology develop, and as social and historical conditions change, theory must and inevitably will change. Engels explicitly pointed out that Marx's entire worldview is not a doctrine but a method; it provides not ready-made dogmas but a point of departure for further study and the method to be used in such study.

Marxism thus fundamentally deconstructs the erroneous tendency to treat grand theory as static dogma. In the history of Marxism's development—from Marx and Engels in the era of free capitalism, to Lenin in the era of imperialism, and on to the Sinicized and contemporized Marxism of our own time—the concrete forms of the theory, its revolutionary strategies, and its programs of construction have undergone several major "changes." Yet this succession of changes has not led to the disintegration of Marxism; on the contrary, it is precisely through the sublation of outdated conclusions in ceaseless historical practice that Marxism has maintained its vigorous vitality.

In sum, whether it is the retreat of Kantian transcendental forms before Quinean holism, or the Williamsonian evolution from folk logic to scientific logic; whether it is the reconstruction of the norms of the sociology of science in the post-academic era, or Marx's materialist overturning of Hegel's Absolute Spirit—the whole history of the evolution of human thought demonstrates that any attempt to construct an absolutely invariant bedrock of truth can scarcely endure. The evolution of theory is a dynamic dialectical process full of tension, and "change" is one of the normal states by which a knowledge system responds to the great transformations of the age and preserves its vitality.

## Cognitive Dispositions and the Theoretical Core

Having deconstructed the "absolute invariance" of transcendental foundationalism, by what, then, is the identity of a theory maintained? This section argues that the "invariance" of a theory is, in essence, not the congealing of static doctrinal articles, but exists as a resilient "defeasible cognitive disposition," as a normative ethos of science, and as the fundamental material basis and value anchor of Marxism. Before unfolding this three-tiered analysis, it is necessary first to address head-on a theoretical difficulty: how does one distinguish the "core" from the "periphery"? Who determines which principle belongs to the "unrevisable core assumptions" and which to the "defeasible auxiliary hypotheses"?

This very difficulty is the question that Quine's "web of belief" leaves suspended—for Quine himself held that there is, in principle, no center or periphery within the web, only pragmatic differences in the cost of revision. But if we rest content with this, no stable structure can be spoken of at all. This paper adopts a combinatorial answer. First, the core is not given a priori but emerges, after the fact, across successive crises as the part most resistant to revision; it consists of those core assumptions that the community, in long-term practice, is in fact unwilling to abandon, just as Lakatos describes the "hard core." Second, and more importantly, the reason the core possesses such resistance to revision is often that it is anchored by deeper value commitments—for example, the cognitive commitment to consistency and explicability, and the value commitment to human emancipation and the common good. The "core," therefore, is not a checklist of certain specific propositions but a stable region jointly marked out by the two dimensions of cognition and value. Although this delimitation cannot entirely eliminate the vagueness of demarcation, it can free the core/periphery distinction from the suspicion of circular argument and provide a basis for the three-tiered analysis that follows.

At the micro level, the core mechanism that maintains the cognitive coherence of the subject is the "defeasible inferential disposition." The acceptance of a theory is not an arbitrary voluntary choice but, like belief, is strictly constrained by evidence. In logical and mathematical practice, underlying laws often function as "general generalizations." Take the third mathematical crisis as an example: in practice, mathematicians generally possess a defeasible disposition to accept the "principle of naïve comprehension," so as to obtain the set delimited by a predicate. Yet when they encountered Russell's paradox—an anomaly that triggers a severe self-referential contradiction—mathematicians did not directly discard the ontology of set theory but, at a subconscious level, revoked this disposition, refusing those "absurd instances" that lead to contradiction. This underlying cognitive disposition, which pursues systemic consistency and explanatory power, constitutes the micro-level resilient bedrock that maintains the theory's identity; the revision of peripheral rules (such as the shift to the ZFC axiom system) is a defensive protection of this invariant core.

The micro-level "cognitive disposition," mapped upward, manifests in the field of the sociology of science as an institutionalized ethos of science. Robert Merton proposed the famous system of scientific norms—usually designated by the acronym CUDOS: communism, universalism, disinterestedness, and organized skepticism. Merton held that these norms possess not only methodological rationality but also moral binding force, as prescriptive technical imperatives. "Universalism" means that the validity of a scientific claim is independent of the social and political status of the one who advances it, its criterion of judgment necessarily following impersonal empirical confirmation and logical consistency; "disinterestedness" stresses that scientists should take the pursuit of objective truth as their highest goal; "communism" requires that scientific knowledge, as a collaborative product, be shared by the whole of society. These basic spirits constitute the founding bedrock of the scientific institution and should remain "invariant" in any age.

Yet upon entering the "post-academic era," characterized by socialization and capitalization, science exhibits the "Mode 2" features of knowledge production: research tends toward collectivization and utility, and there even appears the phenomenon of proprietary knowledge that runs counter to communism. In the face of this alienation and anomaly, the core of the Mertonian norms has not disintegrated but has achieved self-consistency through structural reconstruction. On the premise of safeguarding the "invariant" spirit of scientific autonomy and the growth of objective knowledge, the scientific community, by coordinating mandatory and advocatory norms and supplementing them with external regulatory norms such as "honesty" and "responsibility" (change), buffers the ethical risks brought by the socialization of research. The connotation of "communism," for instance, has expanded from the mere sharing of discovered conclusions to the comprehensive sharing, in open science, of datasets, code, and underlying protocols. This flexible change of peripheral norms is precisely what defends the "invariant" core of science's pursuit of truth in an age of capital penetration.

The resilience of micro-level logical dispositions and meso-level scientific norms is further sublimated within the grand narrative of Marxism. Zhang Jianyun has systematically argued that the method of scientifically grasping the system of basic principles of Marxism must be grounded in the dialectical unity of the view of practice, the materialist view, and the view of value. This unity is not only the framework of the theoretical system but also marks out the core region in which Marxism "is what it is," constituting what this paper calls the locus of "invariance."

First, the Marxist materialist view emphasizes the objective reality of the world of objects (including human beings themselves and human society), which is the precondition and point of departure for all cognition. Unlike old materialism, which reduced matter to atomic entities, dialectical materialism takes materiality as the baseline of epistemology, refusing the idealist slippage that would replace a worldview with epistemology. Second, the view of practice is the primary and basic standpoint of Marxism; it emphasizes purposive, active, creative human activity, which constitutes the source of the motive force by which theory continually absorbs reality. Finally, the view of value establishes the concrete and realistic value orientation of seeking the interests of the overwhelming majority and pursuing human emancipation. Within this unity, the "invariance" of theory is distilled into adherence to this fundamental standpoint and worldview. It permits the "change" of peripheral theories and concrete strategies—as the age develops, the concrete formulations of the basic principles may change, and their connotations are continually enriched in historical practice. This "dialectic of change and invariance" ensures that Marxism neither degenerates into dogmatized, lifeless doctrine nor loses its self-identity amid the changes of the age.

At this point we can see that, from the micro-level inferential disposition, through the meso-level scientific norms, to the macro-level theoretical system, the "invariance" of all three levels shares a common structural feature: none of them is the rigid adherence to certain specific propositions, but rather a stable region jointly marked out by cognitive disposition and value commitment. This structure gives the theoretical system ample room for revision when facing anomalies, without losing its self-identity in the course of that revision.

## "Change" and "Invariance" in the Evolution of Theory

Having deconstructed "absolute invariance" and established the structural resilience of the theoretical core, epistemological inquiry must confront an even more arduous dynamical question: when a mature knowledge system encounters an epochal anomaly or structural crisis capable of overturning its very foundations, how does it avoid total ontological collapse and thereby achieve a spiral self-renewal? This section aims to cross the trap of incommensurability implied in the Kuhnian "paradigm shift" and, on the basis of the dialectical logic of "sublation" and in combination with Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, to construct a structured evolutionary model of "a core anchored and a periphery adapting."

In the history of the philosophy of science, Kuhn's theory of "paradigm shifts" revealed the discontinuity of scientific revolutions, but the thesis of "meaning change" that he maintained together with Feyerabend—namely, the thoroughgoing shift in the meanings of terms under old and new paradigms—to a certain extent led scientific revolution toward the abyss of relativism. On this view there exists an incommensurable logical rupture between old and new theories, which reduces theoretical competition to a mutually unintelligible "change of subject" rather than a genuine scholarly disagreement.

To avert this epistemological risk, this paper draws on Lakatos's "methodology of scientific research programmes" and combines it with "sublation" in the Hegelian–Marxist sense. Lakatos held that a mature scientific research programme consists of two parts: the hard core is the basic assumption that researchers protect by directing the modus tollens away from it (onto the protective belt); the protective belt is the outer layer composed of adjustable auxiliary hypotheses, initial conditions, and observation statements. When the programme encounters an anomaly, researchers will preferentially revise the protective belt rather than shake the hard core—this is not a dogmatic evasion but the methodological precondition that allows the research programme to advance continuously. In drawing on this structure, this paper makes two extensions. First, the hard core is constituted not only by cognitive commitments but also by value commitments (a dimension Lakatos himself did not fully develop). Second, the boundary between hard core and protective belt is manifested historically and after the fact, rather than given a priori—a point closer to dialectical genesis in the Hegelian–Marxist sense. The model thus formed may be stated as follows: when a theory encounters an anomaly, it achieves transformation not by wholly negating the old core, but, through adaptive revision of the system's peripheral auxiliary rules, concrete applications, and external norms (change), by defending and sublimating its core spirit at a higher dimension (invariance). This evolutionary logic ensures that, after undergoing transformation, the theory still preserves both historical and logical continuity.

The evolution of the sociology of science provides macro-level empirical support for this dynamic model. The scientific ethos established by Merton (such as universalism and communism) aims to defend the autonomy of science and the growth of objective knowledge, and this basic spirit constitutes the founding bedrock of the scientific institution. Upon entering the "post-academic era," science exhibits the features of collectivization, utility, and deep entanglement with capital. In the face of this structural challenge the scientific institution has not disintegrated but has achieved self-consistency through the dynamic reconstruction of its normative system—safeguarding scientific autonomy and pursuing truth remain the inalienable institutional bottom line; by coordinating mandatory and advocatory norms within the system and introducing external norms such as "honesty" and "responsibility" (change), it buffers the ethical risks brought by socialization. This "peripheral change" is, in essence, a defensive expansion carried out in order to protect the "invariance of the core."

The "third mathematical crisis" in the history of mathematics provides a typical case at the micro-epistemological level for this model. The impact of Russell's paradox upon the edifice of Fregean logicism did not provoke a wholesale rejection of the ontology of set theory by mathematics, but rather triggered a campaign of systemic self-healing grounded in an underlying "cognitive disposition." It is worth elaborating that, in the face of this crisis, the mathematical community had not just one path of repair but saw several parallel programmes emerge: the type theory proposed by Russell himself, which forbids self-reference by introducing a hierarchy of types; the axiomatic set theory of Zermelo and Fraenkel (ZFC), which excludes the contradiction-producing instances by restricting the principle of comprehension; and the intuitionist path of Brouwer and Heyting, which rebuilds the foundations of mathematics by restricting classical logic (in particular the law of excluded middle). The mathematical community ultimately chose the ZFC route in the mainstream, but type theory retained important influence in computer science and proof theory, and intuitionism has continued to develop in constructive mathematics. This historical fact is profoundly significant: the identification of the "core" is itself the result of historical choice, and different repair programmes gave different answers to the question of what belongs to the core; yet all programmes shared a deeper commitment—to preserve the consistency and explanatory power of mathematics. In other words, the most robust "invariance" is not any specific choice of axioms but the cognitive commitment to "pursuing consistency and explanatory power" itself. This precisely confirms the definition of the "core" given earlier in this paper.

Synthesizing the analysis of the normative succession within the sociology of science and the repair of the logical paradox, we can, under the dual horizons of structural functionalism and analytic philosophy, establish a structured dynamic model of theoretical evolution. In this model, "change" and "invariance" are not a retreat in the sense of a spatial metaphor but a necessary mechanism that a knowledge system generates, when facing empirical anomalies and contextual shifts, in order to eliminate logical contradictions and maintain the overall validity of the system. The identity of a knowledge system does not depend on absolutely rigid dogma but is realized through the logical closed loop of "revising secondary rules so as to maintain the identity of core assumptions": "change" is the logical incorporation of anomalous experience, a strategic local revision; "invariance" is the adherence to underlying principles and the core standpoint. It is precisely within this dynamic tension that a knowledge system is able to establish its own coordinates in an uncertain world and to achieve a spiral self-transcendence.

## Practical Applications and Contemporary Implications

With the dynamic-dialectical model of "change and invariance" in theoretical evolution now established, the ultimate aim of epistemological inquiry must point toward contemporary practice: in a modern world full of upheaval and high uncertainty, how can we avoid the dual trap of dogmatism and relativism and provide a resilient structure—at once flexible and stable—for the iteration of scientific theories, the innovation of social governance, and even the theoretical identity of the individual subject? This section places the model in the real context of rapid knowledge iteration and technological transformation and explores its paths of application and its value for the age.

Dogmatism tends to ossify the concrete conclusions or policies of a particular historical condition into eternal truths, refusing any form of "change." When facing complex and diverse scientific practice, treating the concrete rules of early technological governance as unrevisable dogma leads to a divorce between theory and reality. Classical Marxist theory long ago expounded the concreteness and historicity of truth; refusing the sublation and revocation of secondary rules can only deprive a theory of vitality. Relativism, by contrast, under the appearance that "everything is in flux," abandons any "invariant" core and degrades theoretical identity into a drifting nihilism. In the "post-truth era," algorithmic recommendation and information cocoons amplify this risk, making the subject all too liable to lose the solid cornerstone for establishing belief and collective action.

The model proposed in this paper requires that, while holding fast to the underlying cognitive disposition and basic standpoint (invariance), we make strategic adjustments to concrete applications and external norms (change). Take the problem of AI alignment as an example: its underlying "invariant" core is the practical view that takes "human welfare as the fundamental value standpoint," together with the cognitive disposition that pursues systemic consistency and explicability; the "change" is embodied in the continual iteration of concrete alignment techniques—from early rule-based alignment, to reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), and on to Constitutional AI, and so forth. This structure of "a core anchored and a periphery adapting" both avoids the rigidity of dogmatism and prevents the alienation of relativism.

The logic of "revising secondary rules so as to maintain the identity of core assumptions" provides an operable paradigm of structural design for the construction of contemporary complex systems: any mature institution or theory should embed a resilient architecture of "a stable core and a sensitive periphery." At the level of scientific theory, one must clearly distinguish the "unrevisable core assumptions" (such as the growth of objective knowledge and the scientific autonomy defended by the Mertonian norms) from the "defeasible auxiliary hypotheses." Against the background of scientific and technological self-reliance, the reconstruction of scientific norms has become a pressing task. The core of this process lies in adhering to the invariance of the basic spirit of science—namely, the core aim of safeguarding scientific autonomy and ensuring the continual expansion of objective knowledge—while, by coordinating different types of scientific norms and supplementing them with external norms (change), adapting to the increasingly socialized and capitalized environment of the post-academic era.

In the field of governance, faced with global issues such as climate change and digital sovereignty, decision-makers should not treat the concrete policy instruments of a particular historical stage as dogma. On the contrary, while holding fast to fundamental value standpoints such as "a community with a shared future for humankind," they should permit the dynamic optimization of concrete governance paths and mechanisms of international cooperation. Only by maintaining this tension between inner and outer can theoretical identity be transformed into a lasting force of social practice.

For the younger generation, this model carries a profound existential significance. It reminds us that theoretical identity is neither rigid recitation nor arbitrary deconstruction, but the establishment, within a dynamic tension, of a resilient spiritual coordinate. In the modern liquid society characterized by rapid knowledge iteration and technological transformation, epistemological anxiety and the crisis of value identity are ubiquitous shadows. The dynamic dialectic of "change" and "invariance" establishes for us a highly resilient theoretical coordinate system, helping to avert the dual trap of dogmatism and relativism. This is precisely the core purport of philosophical reflection—to hold fast, normatively, to the "invariance" of the core amid the free exploration of the "change" of the periphery. The identity of a knowledge system and of theoretical conviction depends neither on the rote memorization of absolutely rigid dogma, nor need it drift aimlessly toward nihilism in the flood of information.

From Kant to Quine and Williamson, from Merton to Lakatos, from Hegel to Marx, human reason's inquiry into the problem of "change and invariance" has passed through a spirally deepening dialectical process. Through three parallel theoretical paths—analytic philosophy's deconstruction of the apriority of logic, the sociology of science's reconstruction of normative stability, and Marxism's critical inheritance of Hegel's closed dialectic—and by integrating them with Lakatos's hard-core/protective-belt structure, this paper has revealed the unified structure to which they all point: hierarchical resilience. This structure shows that, whether at the level of micro inferential dispositions, meso scientific norms, or macro theoretical systems, "change" and "invariance" have never been a simple binary opposition but a dialectical unity within dynamic tension—"change" is the logical incorporation of anomalous experience, a strategic local revision; "invariance" is the adherence to underlying principles and the core standpoint. It is precisely within this dynamic tension that a knowledge system is able to establish its own coordinates in an uncertain world and to achieve a spiral self-transcendence. This dialectical model is at once a descriptive summary of the laws of the evolution of human thought and a normative guide for re-establishing theoretical identity in liquid modernity.
