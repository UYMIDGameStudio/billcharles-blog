## Abstract

In what sense can a body of knowledge continue after its language, tools, and even its proof environment have changed? I previously answered this question by distinguishing a theory’s core from its periphery and treating theoretical continuity as the preservation of core commitments amid peripheral revision. This article revises that account. Structural centrality can show how much of a present system depends on a given element, while methodological protection can explain why researchers are reluctant to revise certain assumptions. Neither, however, can by itself establish a historical relation between two stages of inquiry. Yet the alternative notion of epistemic uptake is too broad: researchers can cite, translate, and use the results of another tradition without becoming its successors.

Rather than offer a single identity criterion for every knowledge tradition, I examine a narrower relation: migration-based epistemic succession. Here an earlier body of work is explicitly made the object of migration, and what is migrated includes results that already possess epistemic standing, not merely files, interfaces, or behavioral outputs. Those results must acquire standing as usable knowledge under a new proof environment and new standards of acceptance. The earlier stage’s role as a completion benchmark is strong evidence for this relation, but it is neither necessary for every kind of epistemic succession nor sufficient to turn an ordinary software compatibility project into one.

The migration of mathlib from Lean 3 to Lean 4 makes this relation unusually visible. Lean 4 reimplemented much of the prover’s infrastructure, so old source code, tactics, and metaprograms could not simply be carried over. At the same time, mathport, declaration alignments, porting notes, and status records continued to take mathlib3 as their point of reference. Old theorems could be reproved or absorbed into more general results; interfaces and tactics could be replaced; but mathematical claims still had to regain acceptable proof status in the new environment. On this basis, I distinguish engineering migration, epistemic succession, and epistemic appraisal. Argumentative responsibility is not part of the definition of succession: a live counterexample must be addressed because it still bears on present reasoning, not because historical inheritance creates a new basic epistemic duty.

*Keywords:* diachronic continuity; knowledge migration; epistemic succession; argumentative responsibility; mathlib; Lean

## Introduction

We do not usually say that a mathematical theorem becomes a different theorem merely because it is written in a new notation. Nor do we always treat a theory as a new historical object once it abandons some of its earlier assumptions. This tolerance has limits. Some changes count as revision, migration, or reconstruction; others mark the end of the earlier work. What grounds the distinction?

Formalized mathematics offers a particularly clear case. Lean is an interactive theorem prover in which mathematicians and developers write definitions, theorem statements, and proofs in a formal language; Lean elaborates this input into objects that its kernel can check (de Moura and Ullrich 625-26). The mathlib Community describes mathlib as a community-driven formal library for Lean, developed to support both established and research-level mathematics (367-68). It contains mathematics in algebra, analysis, topology, and many other fields, but it also contains notation, automated tactics, metaprograms, and the software structure that organizes them. For a mathematical result to enter mathlib, it must be expressed as a formal object that Lean can accept and check, and it must be able to enter dependency relations with other results in the library.

Lean 4 subjected this library to a far-reaching technical change. Leonardo de Moura and Sebastian Ullrich describe Lean 4 as a reimplementation of Lean. It redesigned such infrastructure as parsing, macros, elaboration, tactics, and metaprogramming, while also turning Lean into an efficient functional programming language (625-26, 630-33). For mathlib, this was more than a change of version number. Lean 3 source files could not continue to work through ordinary backward compatibility; some tactics and metaprograms had to be reimplemented, while source code required both automated conversion and manual repair (Morrison).

The community developed mathport for this purpose. Scott Morrison divides the tool into two components. binport constructs Lean 4 .olean files from Lean 3 .olean files; synport uses those outputs together with Lean 3 abstract syntax trees to generate the best Lean 4 source code it can. The result was always meant to be the beginning of further human work, not a perfect source-to-source translation. By July 2023, the mathlib4 “Porting Wiki” reported that “Every file has been ported.” This yields a fact worth philosophical attention. As a software object, mathlib underwent a large-scale replacement of its implementation. Yet those carrying out the migration did not understand themselves to be building a new mathematical library unrelated to mathlib3.

The shared name cannot settle the matter. A continuous GitHub organization, overlapping maintainers, and an explicit version history explain administrative continuity, but not what happened to the mathematics. Nor is the reuse of old mathematical content sufficient. A new formalization project may borrow a theorem from mathlib, acknowledge the source, and check the theorem again. That act of borrowing does not make the project a successor to mathlib.

There is a second contrast. An ordinary software rewrite can also treat the earlier version as a migration checklist: a missing interface counts as a defect, and a failed compatibility test prevents completion. If using an earlier system as a completion benchmark were enough for epistemic succession, then a contractually specified API-compatibility project would fall under the same concept. A completion benchmark distinguishes systematic takeover from isolated borrowing, but it does not by itself show that the object being taken over is knowledge rather than a software specification.

This article therefore examines migration-based epistemic succession. In this relation, an earlier epistemic practice is explicitly made the object of migration, and a later practice systematically re-establishes the present epistemic standing of earlier results in a changed medium of expression, proof, or verification. “Epistemic standing” is not a mysterious property. In mathlib, its clearest instance is simple: if a theorem accepted in the old library is to remain available as a theorem in the new library, it must acquire an acceptable counterpart under Lean 4’s formal environment and kernel checking. Copying a file, retaining an API name, or reproducing an output does not substitute for this requirement.

The continued treatment of mathlib3 content as unfinished work in mathlib4 is strong evidence of a migration relation. Yet completion benchmarks do not define epistemic succession in general. They also occur in purely technical migration, and they may be absent from transformative successions that continue a tradition by changing its problem space. Such cases matter to the history of knowledge, but the mathlib case does not resolve them. It is better to mark this limit than to force distinct kinds of historical continuation into a single comprehensive definition.

Historical succession must also be kept apart from epistemic appraisal. A tradition may genuinely take over earlier work yet mishandle counterexamples and misjudge proofs; it would then be a poor successor, not necessarily a non-successor. Conversely, an outside critic may refute an earlier theory with great care without taking over its research. A counterexample requires an answer when it still bears on the justification of a present claim. The requirement does not arise because a personified “tradition” places its descendants in debt. Historical relations shape which materials enter present work and which evidence is reasonably available to researchers; they do not create a separate epistemic law.

The argument proceeds in three steps. I first separate structural centrality, methodological protection, and historical succession. I then distinguish migration-based succession from the broader relation of epistemic uptake. Finally, I use the migration of mathlib to show why the decisive issue is not how completely old files were moved but how the epistemic standing of mathematical claims was re-established across a new technical environment. The analysis does not answer when every possible knowledge system dies. It isolates one important kind of diachronic continuity that is neither the survival of an invariant entity, nor ordinary software compatibility, nor the accumulation of citations.

## From Structural Cores to Historical Relations

A basic definition on which thousands of results depend does not occupy the same structural position as an isolated lemma with no downstream use. When changing an element would force the reconstruction of a large body of existing reasoning, that element has a high degree of counterfactual dependence. Its cost of revision will usually be high as well.

Trouble begins when this synchronic property is made to explain diachronic continuity. The clearest circularity arises when the “core” is identified only after change has occurred. Suppose that A survives while B and C disappear. If A is called the core because it survived, and the theory’s continuity is then explained by the survival of its core, the explanation merely renames the observed survival.

This objection has a limited target. If researchers independently designate A as a research program’s core before the change and later continue to protect it, there is no definitional circle. The questions are then different: is the preservation of A sufficient for historical succession, and must succession end if A is abandoned? These questions cannot be settled by pointing to circularity. They require us to ask whether core preservation and historical relation can come apart.

Quine and Lakatos each have a limited role here. In “Two Dogmas of Empiricism,” Quine presents our beliefs as a “man-made fabric” and a “field of force” whose boundary conditions are experience. He does not deny every difference of degree between center and periphery. Some statements lie closer to the experiential boundary; others are deeply embedded in the network. What holism removes is a fixed line of a priori immunity, not centrality as such (42-43). My earlier attempt to introduce Lakatos on the ground that Quine’s picture lacked structure therefore rested on a false premise (Wang).

Lakatos need not disappear from the discussion. His negative heuristic describes a deliberate policy of protecting a research program’s hard core and directing adjustments toward auxiliary hypotheses in the protective belt (48-50). This differs from structural dependence. A proposition may be difficult to revise because too much else depends on it; it may also be left unrevised because researchers decide, at a particular stage, not to direct criticism toward it. The first concerns a position in a network. The second concerns a research strategy.

Both explanations, however, stop at the same boundary. They answer why a particular element is not being changed now. They do not answer why later work counts as a continuation once change has occurred. Imagine two isolated mathematical groups that independently adopt equivalent axioms and arrive at highly isomorphic theories. The two theories may even have identical structural cores. We would still have no historical reason to call the second group the successor of the first. Structural similarity has no direction.

The reverse case is equally possible. A tradition may abandon a heavily protected assumption A while continuing to work on questions generated by the theory in which A once figured. Some results may be reproved and others explicitly withdrawn. The loss of A marks a profound structural change, but it does not by itself show that the later work is historically unrelated to what came before. To judge that relation, we must know which pieces of work were actually taken up.

Nor does redefining the core as “the part most expensive to alter” bridge the gap. A badly designed legacy interface may be extremely difficult to replace because too much depends on it. An inefficient administrative routine may acquire enormous adjustment costs merely by operating for decades. Such costs indicate path dependence and burdens of coordination; they do not automatically confer epistemic value. Revision cost can help measure dependence within a present structure. It cannot establish that a difficult-to-change element must carry the system’s identity through history.

I therefore retain structural centrality and methodological protection as useful concepts while removing from them the task of defining diachronic identity. “Most difficult to change,” “deliberately protected,” and “continued by later work” are judgments that may correlate but cannot define one another.

## Migration-Based Epistemic Succession

Let P2 know that a result T came from P1. P2 may cite T, translate it, or even prove it anew in another formal system. If T plays a role in P2’s current reasoning, this is genuine epistemic uptake rather than archival preservation. It still need not amount to succession. A research program can borrow results from dozens of traditions without becoming the joint successor of all of them.

Systematic takeover is stronger than isolated borrowing. If P2 treats the established scope of P1 as a migration specification and checks item by item what has been handled and what remains missing, P1 is no longer merely a source of material. It also helps determine when P2 may declare the task complete. An earlier version of this argument moved too quickly from such a completion benchmark to epistemic succession. Compatibility engineering, digital preservation, and replication can exhibit the same structure. A team hired to rewrite old software may be required to provide a counterpart for every API. The resulting checklist is exacting, but the checklist alone does not show that the team has taken over an epistemic practice.

To determine whether knowledge has been migrated, we must ask what role the objects of migration play in later work. A software interface may count as compatible because it returns the same values, and a file may count as preserved because its bytes remain intact. Knowledge enters a later practice as claims, reasons, problems, and results available for further inference. The distinction is especially clear in mathlib. The name of an old theorem may change, its proof script may be rewritten, and a standalone declaration may even disappear. But if the corresponding mathematical content is still to be used as an established result, it must acquire acceptable proof status in the new formal environment.

I call this migration-based epistemic succession. A set of results or problems established in P1 is explicitly placed within P2’s migration scope; P2’s task is not to preserve P1’s material objects but to restore the current epistemic function of that content after a change in the medium of expression, proof, or verification. P1’s completion state will ordinarily serve as a task reference for P2, so omissions appear as gaps. This is a common and powerful empirical mark of the relation, not a metaphysical condition that every conceivable successor must satisfy.

Why does this definition not absorb ordinary software migration? The difference lies not in whether there is a checklist but in what the checklist tracks and what counts as completion. A compatibility project may be completed by satisfying interface behavior, performance constraints, or a contract. In migration-based epistemic succession, at least some tracked objects have propositional or problem-level epistemic functions: the later practice treats them as accepted results, premises in current reasoning, or live problems. Engineering mechanisms can carry this migration but cannot replace it. For a theorem, successful file conversion is only a technical condition. Whether the theorem can be accepted as a theorem in the new environment determines whether its earlier epistemic standing has a counterpart.

The domain D in which this relation is asserted also needs an independent boundary. Project modules, directory structures, and task lists can help show that an analyst has not narrowed the domain only after seeing the result. Yet an administrative boundary does not thereby become an epistemic domain. D must also be supported by relations among the contents: for example, a set of declarations may concern the same mathematical objects, stand in proof dependencies, or jointly serve a mathematical task identifiable before migration. Software modules are historical evidence, not epistemic boundaries in their own right. When engineering and content boundaries diverge, the analysis should follow the actual epistemic relations rather than let a repository directory decide the philosophical classification.

Unsolved problems likewise cannot be treated as continuous merely because participants say they are still working on “the same problem.” Names may persist through institutional inertia even when a new problem Q′ has changed the object, the conditions of failure, and the standards of success. Participants’ self-understanding remains important evidence, but it must be checked against the structure of the problem. Does Q′ still address an identifiable difficulty in Q? Would solving Q′ alter how Q is treated? After conceptual change, can researchers say which part of the old problem was retained, cancelled, or transformed? These relations keep problem continuity from collapsing into self-description.

This restriction deliberately leaves transformative succession outside the present account. The history of science may contain a different kind of continuation in which later researchers clearly emerge from an earlier tradition and continue to use its results and training lineage while a conceptual revolution changes what counts as a problem and what counts as success. The unfinished items of the earlier tradition may no longer determine the later project’s completion state one by one. We may still have good reason to call the later tradition a successor. The present case supplies neither the evidence nor the need to show that this relation has the structure of migration-based succession. Recognizing different kinds of succession is more honest than abstracting the experience of porting mathlib into a universal ontology of knowledge.

For the same reason, the argument does not directly decide whether an entire knowledge system remains numerically identical. A local result can be migrated; one problem domain can be taken over while another is abandoned; one predecessor can have several later continuations. Migration-based epistemic succession yields domain-relative genealogical judgments. It does not compress every local relation into a score and pronounce the whole system “the same” or “dead.” Such a system-level judgment would require an additional rule for aggregation. I do not have a non-arbitrary one and will not conceal that absence behind terms such as “substantial” or “widespread.”

## From mathlib3 to mathlib4

Porting status records, alignments, porting notes, and the statement “Every file has been ported” establish first that there was a deliberate migration project. An ordinary software rewrite could leave the same kinds of records. The philosophical question is what this project migrated in addition to software, and how results accepted in the old mathematical library acquired corresponding standing in the new environment.

Lean 4’s technical break provides a useful separation. De Moura and Ullrich call it a reimplementation of Lean and describe its new macro system, elaborator, metaprogramming facilities, and runtime design (625-26, 630-33). For mathlib3, the consequence was that old source code could not become valid in Lean 4 merely because it had once compiled. Proof scripts, tactic calls, and metaprograms often required conversion or rewriting.

The design of mathport brings the distinction into focus. binport processes compiled Lean 3 .olean information, while synport uses that information together with Lean 3’s abstract syntax trees to generate Lean 4 source code on a best-effort basis. Morrison explicitly presents synport as a starting point for subsequent human work rather than a perfect translator. When processing declarations, mathport also checks whether mathlib4 already contains a definitionally equal declaration. If it does, the existing counterpart can be used instead of producing a mechanical copy. The object of migration therefore cannot be individuated from the outset as “the same source code.”

Different source code does not relax the verification requirement placed on mathematical declarations. A theorem accepted in mathlib3 does not become an available theorem in mathlib4 because its name has been copied. The converted statement and its proof must be accepted in Lean 4’s formal environment before the result can enter the library and support downstream declarations. This is where the contrast with ordinary interface compatibility becomes substantive. What must be recovered for a theorem is not only external behavior but the status “this proposition has an acceptable proof in the present formal system.” A new proof term may differ entirely from the old one, while later users still treat the corresponding proposition as an established mathematical result.

Only in this context does the completion benchmark acquire epistemic significance. Morrison’s 2021 account describes a project of translating statements at scale while managing alignments between Lean 3 and mathlib3 on one side and Lean 4 and mathlib4 on the other. The 2023 “Porting Wiki” incorporates file status, successful builds and linting, naming conventions, and porting notes for nontrivial changes into the completion process. A missing file appears as an engineering gap. A changed, merged, or omitted declaration must also receive an intelligible destination within the migration.

Taken alone, these checklists establish only that mathlib4 was intended as a comprehensive migration of mathlib3. Joined to the proof status of theorems in the new environment, they show migration-based epistemic succession. The old library identifies a range of objects that must be handled, and some of these objects are not software interfaces whose behavior merely needs to be reproduced. They are mathematical claims that must again become acceptable and dependable results in a new proof environment. Engineering completion and knowledge migration interlock here without becoming the same concept.

The “Porting Wiki” example of Subtype.val_eq_coe shows why restoring epistemic standing does not require preserving every declaration as an object. Under Lean 4’s elaboration of coercions, the old lemma is no longer needed as an independent theorem: the relevant expressions are syntactically identical. A comparison of declaration lists would register a missing theorem. A comparison of the work the theorem once performed yields a better description: the new environment has absorbed the relation, which no longer requires a separate lemma. This is not forgetting but absorption.

The treatment of bit0, bit1, and nthLe reveals the same distinction. The “Porting Wiki” records that bit0 and bit1 were deprecated, while lemmas involving them still had to be ported. nthLe was replaced by get through a substantial API change; porters could refactor to the new interface where appropriate, leave a porting note and a deprecated form of the old lemma, or postpone refactoring until after the port. The goal was not to make the old API inviolable. It was to ensure a traceable disposition for the old mathematical content in the new environment.

Tactics separate continuity of implementation from continuity of knowledge even more sharply. Morrison notes that mathport did not attempt to translate the many tactics written for mathlib3 because metaprogramming in Lean 4 was too different. The relevant infrastructure had to be rebuilt, and foundational parts of the mathematical library sometimes had to be ported by hand before automation such as ring could be restored. Tactics are important tools of proof, but rewriting a tool need not destroy the mathematical tasks for which it was used. Re-establishing old results as verifiable results under the new toolchain is part of what the migration did.

These materials support a limited conclusion. They decisively rule out the preservation of source code, declaration names, or implementation details as necessary conditions of this migration’s epistemic continuity. They also show that mathlib3 was not merely one external source among others for mathlib4. The positive point is that the standing of old mathematical claims had to be re-established in the new system rather than inherited automatically from a project name. The materials do not show that migration is the essence of every form of epistemic succession, nor can a porting checklist alone exclude every non-epistemic instance of systematic migration.

That is the proper evidential limit of a single case. mathlib does not supply a universal ontology of knowledge systems. It answers a smaller question with unusual clarity: when the technical vehicle changes extensively, continuity of mathematical knowledge does not require the numerical identity of old objects. In a formal mathematical library, it can instead be achieved by re-establishing the epistemic standing of earlier results under a new proof environment.

## After Succession: Argumentative Responsibility, Failure, and Rupture

Once migration-based succession has been specified, argumentative responsibility no longer needs to carry the weight of an identity theory. Suppose that a theorem T holds only under condition R and that a researcher uses T to support conclusion C. As long as R affects the support that T gives C, the researcher cannot ignore R. It makes no fundamental difference whether T was inherited or independently discovered. The requirement belongs to present justification, not to a special law of inheritance created by historical lineage.

Provenance can still affect a concrete judgment because it affects the availability of information. If researchers knowingly take T from source A and A prominently records restriction R, they have more reason to be aware of R than they would have of a counterexample buried in an unrelated archive. The force here comes from provenance, reasonable availability, and the researchers’ roles, not from the label “successor.” An ordinary borrower who uses T under the same conditions faces the same requirement. Successor status has no unique normative power.

The state of the evidence must also be distinguished from attributability. That R in fact weakens C is a judgment about the present evidential relation. Whether a particular researcher is blameworthy for failing to address R additionally depends on whether R was reasonably available to that person. If the relevant archive has been lost or the material could not have been found under normal research conditions, C may remain insufficiently supported, but the evidential flaw alone does not establish culpable neglect. “Failure of responsibility” is appropriate only when the researcher’s role, resources, and conditions of inquiry made R reasonably knowable and the researcher nevertheless ignored it without adequate reason.

Unsolved problems present a different case. If P1 leaves problem Q unresolved, not everyone who uses one of P1’s results thereby acquires a duty to solve Q. Earlier failed approaches and known constraints become relevant to appraisal only when later researchers place Q—or a Q′ whose lineage from Q can be shown—on their own agenda. Responsibility for the justification of a result and responsibility for the selection and conduct of a research agenda are different normative questions. They should not be merged merely because both have historical sources.

Formalized mathematics permits one further local distinction. Mathematical propositions, particular proofs, and implementation tools can be separated with relative clarity. When an old theorem receives a new proof in Lean 4, techniques peculiar to the old proof need not remain epistemic burdens. Tactics and metaprograms may also be replaced so long as a new proof chain allows the proposition to regain acceptable status. This three-level distinction reflects the architecture of formalized mathematics; I do not project it onto experimental or social science as a general ontology. In those fields, instruments, measurement operations, and propositional content may be much more tightly entangled.

Nor is the category of a “failed successor” without limits. Suppose the old result is a proposition conditioned by R, while the later practice deletes R and uses the old name to claim that it is applying the same result. We cannot indefinitely describe this as the poor inheritance of an unchanged result. If the propositional content has changed so far that it no longer provides a counterpart to the earlier result, the local migration relation itself may have broken. Distinguishing succession from epistemic appraisal does not mean that every semantic alteration preserves succession.

Rupture in migration-based succession should be understood at the same limited scale. If, within a specified mathematical domain, earlier results no longer seek present counterparts; if earlier problems are not taken into later tasks; and if restoring the epistemic standing of those results ceases to be a migration objective, then the basis for this migration relation has disappeared. Continuous project names, repositories, and personnel cannot replace the abandoned epistemic relation. Conversely, changes in source code, tactics, and maintainers do not by themselves produce rupture while the earlier mathematical work continues to be taken up.

This is not a sufficient theory of the death of an entire knowledge system. A large tradition may undergo migration-based succession in algebra while leaving only archives in another domain; it may also continue in transformative ways not analyzed here. Inferring that “the whole system is still the same” from several local successor relations would require an account of how different domains are weighted and aggregated. I have no non-arbitrary rule for doing so and do not present local genealogy as a verdict of numerical identity at the system level.

Likewise, the failure of static proposition sets, material vehicles, and fixed cores to explain succession does not prove that the true ontology of a knowledge system is a “structure of practice.” That conclusion would again outrun the argument. An adequate study of knowledge through time must at least consider how results regain epistemic functions in later work, how problems are taken up, and how different levels of technical vehicle carry these relations. The broader social ontology to which these relations belong remains a question for another paper.

## Conclusion

My earlier account placed structural cores and diachronic identity within one framework and tried to explain continuity through the preservation of a core. That approach conflated synchronic structure with historical relation. Quinean centrality can explain why some beliefs have wider ramifications. Lakatosian methodological protection can explain why researchers choose not to revise some assumptions for the time being. Whether later work is continuing an earlier body of work remains a different judgment.

Historically mediated epistemic uptake does not settle the issue either. Researchers may know where a result came from, translate it, verify it, and use it while remaining borrowers. An earlier version of this argument tried to close the gap by making the earlier work a completion benchmark for the later. This relation is indeed stronger than borrowing, but it also occurs in software compatibility, preservation, and contract-based reimplementation, and it may be absent from transformative successions that alter the space of problems. A completion benchmark should therefore not be treated as a general necessary condition of epistemic succession.

The more defensible claim concerns migration-based epistemic succession. mathlib3 did serve as the migration reference for mathlib4, so untreated files and declarations entered the engineering process as gaps. If the analysis stopped there, however, it would establish only comprehensive migration. The epistemically distinctive feature is that the objects of migration included declarations already accepted as mathematical results. To remain available as theorems in mathlib4, they needed acceptable proof counterparts under Lean 4’s new formal environment. Source code could be rewritten, tactics replaced, and declarations absorbed by new representational arrangements. What continued was the standing of mathematical results in current reasoning, not every old object that had once carried them.

Counterexamples still require treatment when they bear on current reasoning. Whether researchers are responsible for missing them depends on reasonable availability given their roles and resources. Historical genealogy, present justification, and attributability can therefore be analyzed separately, without saying that a knowledge system is a person-like subject accountable to its own past.

The final claim is deliberately narrow. In migration settings within formalized mathematics, diachronic continuity can survive extensive replacement of technical implementation. One of its central mechanisms is the re-establishment of the epistemic standing of existing mathematical results under a new proof environment. Completion benchmarks reveal the systematic character of the migration; renewed certification of proofs and declarations explains why it is more than software compatibility. This paper does not address transformative successions that change the problems, standards, and objects of inquiry, nor does it explain how several local successor relations compose the historical identity of an entire knowledge system.
